# Stranded with Numbers
*An Islander's Guide to Reinventing Mathematics*

## Reconstructing mathematics from remembered arithmetic
*An island story and a mathematical notebook*

**[Begin reading: Introduction — What Remains](INTRODUCTION.md)**

Suppose we were stranded on a desert island remembering how to count, add, subtract, multiply, and divide—but unable to explain why any of it works. We vaguely recall hearing words like sine, cosine, angles, geometry, and linear equations—but remember no definitions, formulas, identities, or worked examples. We have no textbook, no calculator, and certainly no working knowledge of calculus.

**Could we justify the arithmetic we remember and use it to reconstruct the mathematics of continuous change?**

That is our challenge.

We begin with arithmetic we can perform but cannot justify. Why does repeated addition behave like multiplication? Why does subtraction undo addition? Why do fractions obey their familiar rules? By testing these ideas against concrete problems, we recover their explanations. And by working with increasingly smaller fractions, we learn to calculate things that initially seem impossible to measure.

From these elementary operations, we gradually develop ways to describe exponential growth, circular motion, changing velocities, and the accumulation of quantities over time. Rather than beginning with established formulas, we discover them through numerical experiments, recognize the patterns they reveal, and develop methods for calculating them more precisely.

Our ambition is to **reconstruct calculus without assuming its familiar functions, formulas, or methods in advance.**

Euler's identity will be our guiding destination:

$$
e^{i\pi}+1=0
$$

Not because we already know what $e$, $i$, or $\pi$ mean, but because we want to discover how these seemingly unrelated numbers arise from elementary arithmetic and why they fit together so naturally.

Along the way, we will find that multiplication can describe both growth and rotation, that fractions can approximate continuous change, and that increasingly precise calculations can reveal mathematical relationships far beyond the operations with which we began.

**Our governing rule is simple: Never introduce a new mathematical idea before showing why we need it and how we might construct it from what we already know.**

We will begin with remembered arithmetic, justify its rules, proceed through numerical approximations, and introduce more sophisticated mathematics only when our existing tools prove insufficient. The goal is not merely to learn calculus, but to understand how we could have discovered it ourselves.
