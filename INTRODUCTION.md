# Introduction — What Remains

On the first morning, he counted what the tide had left him.

Seven pieces of driftwood. Three lengths of rope, none particularly long. Two containers that might hold rainwater. One knife.

He could count. That much was certain.

He could also add the seven pieces of wood to the three he found farther up the beach and arrive at ten. He could multiply seven by three without arranging twenty-one objects in the sand. He could divide twelve by four and get three. These things came to him as readily as words.

But when he tried to explain *why* they worked, he found that he knew less than he thought.

Why should three groups of seven contain the same number of objects as seven groups of three? Why was dividing by a fraction the same as multiplying by its reciprocal? He remembered doing both, perhaps hundreds of times, but could not remember a reason.

He drew a line in the sand and marked a point at either end. Somewhere in his memory were other words: *geometry*, *angles*, *sine*, *cosine*. There had been something about triangles. Something about *linear equations*. He could not say what any of it meant. No definitions, no diagrams, no identities came back to him. Only the names.

The sea had taken his books, if he had ever had any. It had not taken his ability to ask questions.

He found a flat stone, a piece of charcoal, and a dry place above the reach of the tide. At the top of the stone he wrote:

## Notebook — Day One

**What I can do**

- Count things and compare quantities.
- Add, subtract, multiply, and divide.
- Work with ordinary fractions, at least when the arithmetic is familiar.

**What I cannot yet explain**

- Why the rules of arithmetic are true.
- How to measure a distance I cannot walk, or an area I cannot cover with equal squares.
- How to describe a direction or a turn with numbers.
- How to calculate something that changes while I am measuring it.
- What the half-remembered words *sine*, *cosine*, and *linear equation* refer to.

**First rule**

> A remembered procedure is not an explanation.

**Second rule**

> When a new idea becomes necessary, construct it from things already understood. Do not borrow a formula merely because its name is familiar.

**Third rule**

> A calculation can suggest a pattern. It cannot, by itself, prove that the pattern always holds.

He looked again at the two marks in the sand. Between them was a distance. He could estimate it by laying the rope along the ground, moving it end to end, and counting. But what if the points were on opposite sides of a ravine? What if he wanted to know the distance to a ship beyond the reef? What if the quantity he needed was not a length at all, but the speed of the tide at a particular instant?

He knew enough arithmetic to begin. He did not yet know enough mathematics to answer.

That would have to change.

## The undertaking

*Stranded with Numbers* follows a castaway who remembers elementary arithmetic but has forgotten its foundations and almost everything beyond it. The island supplies problems. His notebook supplies experiments, conjectures, constructions, and—eventually—proofs.

We will work beside him. We may use counting, addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and familiar fractions from the outset, but we will not take their laws for granted. When we encounter an unfamiliar idea, we will first try to build it with finite calculations and concrete examples. We will name it only when we understand what it does.

As the notebook grows, so will its reach: from arithmetic to patterns; from patterns to geometry and rotation; from repeated growth to exponential behavior; and from measurements over finite intervals to calculus. Numerical evidence will come before formal theory, but we will keep careful track of what remains unproved.

One distant landmark is the extraordinary equation

$$
e^{i\pi}+1=0.
$$

For now, its symbols are mostly strangers. That is precisely the point. We want to arrive at this equation not by memorizing it, but by earning the meaning of every part.

The journey begins with a simpler question: **What do we actually know when we say that two and three make five?**
