# Chapter 01 — One Thing, Two Things

*Act I: Counting the Island*

The next morning he carried the two containers to a pool above the beach.

One was narrow and tall. The other was broad and shallow. He filled them both, looked at them, and realized that he did not know which held more water.

He knew how to count. But what, precisely, was he supposed to count?

The tall container looked larger from one side; the broad one looked larger from another. He could count *containers*: one and one. That would tell him nothing about their contents.

He poured water from the first container into a shell, emptied the shell, and repeated. Five full shells and a little left over. From the second he obtained six full shells, provided he filled the shell to the same mark each time.

He had made a measurement. But the answer depended on the shell.

He set the shell beside his notebook.

> Before I count, I must decide what counts as **one**.

## Notebook 01.1 — Choosing a unit

A number by itself does not tell us how much water we have. We must specify what one counted thing represents.

For pieces of wood, the unit might be **one piece**. For water, it might be **one shellful**. For distance, it might be **one rope-length**.

A unit must remain the same throughout a comparison. If the shell is filled to different levels, the numbers cannot be trusted.

**Experiment.** Suppose a container holds exactly six shellfuls. Another holds four. We may say the first holds two more *shellfuls*, but we cannot conclude it holds two more *containers*.

The number and its unit belong together.

### Checkpoint A

A pile contains eight short sticks. Another contains five long sticks. Does the first pile contain more *wood*?

*Not enough information.* We know it contains more **sticks**, but the sticks are not equal units of wood.

## Notebook 01.2 — What counting actually does

He laid five equal stones in a row.

\`\`\`text
●  ●  ●  ●  ●
1  2  3  4  5
\`\`\`

The final word, *five*, named the number of stones. The stones did not need to be in a row. He could move them into a circle and count again.

What had remained unchanged?

Each stone was still present, and none had been counted twice. Counting paired each stone with one successive counting word. Moving the stones did not create or destroy any of them.

He wrote:

**Counting rule.** If every object is counted exactly once, rearranging the objects does not change the total.

This is more than a habit. It is a claim about matching objects: after the rearrangement, each original stone corresponds to exactly one stone in the new arrangement.

There is a limitation. A pile of five stones and a pile of five shells have the same **number of objects**, not the same weight, size, or usefulness.

## Notebook 01.3 — Why addition works

He placed two stones to the left of a line and three to the right.

\`\`\`text
● ●   |   ● ● ●
\`\`\`

Removing the line gave one collection of five stones.

\`\`\`text
● ● ● ● ●
\`\`\`

His memory supplied the familiar expression:

\[
2+3=5.
\]

But now he could explain what it meant: the two collections contain no shared stones, and together they contain five.

The condition matters. If he counts the *same* stone in both groups, adding the two counts would count that stone twice.

**Addition of counts** combines collections that do not overlap.

### An unexpected question

He moved the groups around.

\`\`\`text
● ● ●   |   ● ●
\`\`\`

This time he counted three first, then two. There were still five stones.

So:

\[
2+3=3+2.
\]

The arithmetic rule had a physical explanation: exchanging the positions of two separate groups changes neither group nor their combined collection.

He remembered that this property had a name. He did not need the name to see why it was true.

*Later label:* **commutativity of addition**.

## Notebook 01.4 — Does grouping matter?

He divided six stones into three groups:

\`\`\`text
●   |   ● ●   |   ● ● ●
\`\`\`

He could combine the first two groups, then the third:

\[
(1+2)+3=6.
\]

Or combine the last two, then the first:

\[
1+(2+3)=6.
\]

In both cases he ended with the same six stones. The intermediate piles differed, but the final collection did not.

Thus, for counts of separate groups:

\[
(a+b)+c=a+(b+c).
\]

The letters do not introduce a mysterious new operation. They stand for any three counts of separate groups. We have given a general reason, rather than checking just one set of numbers: either grouping combines exactly the same objects.

*Later label:* **associativity of addition**.

## Notebook 01.5 — What we have, and what we have not proved

He returned to the water containers. The shell had helped him compare them, but only because he had kept the unit fixed. The stones had helped him justify addition, but only because he had counted each one once.

He made two columns.

| Established for finite collections | Not yet established |
|---|---|
| Counting requires identifying what counts as one | How to compare quantities with no natural separate pieces |
| Rearranging objects preserves their count | Why a numerical measurement can be approximate |
| Counts of disjoint collections can be added | How to represent an empty collection as a number |
| Changing the order of two groups does not change their total | Why subtraction undoes addition |
| Changing the grouping of three groups does not change their total | Why multiplication and division obey their familiar rules |

The distinction was useful. He had explained something, but not everything. Even the water experiment relied on a shell that could leave a remainder smaller than one full measure.

## The reader's challenge

You have three separate piles containing two, four, and five stones.

1. Explain why \((2+4)+5\) and \(2+(4+5)\) must give the same count **without** merely calculating both totals.
2. One stone is accidentally included in two piles. Why can adding the recorded pile counts now give a wrong answer?
3. You count seven pieces of firewood and your companion counts nine. What further information would you need before concluding your companion has more *wood*?

**Mastery test:** Can you explain, using collections rather than a memorized rule, why changing the order or grouping of additions preserves the total?
